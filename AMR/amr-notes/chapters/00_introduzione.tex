\chapter{Introduction: Applications, Problems, Architectures}
\label{ch:introduction}

This introductory chapter frames the course. It does not introduce
technical tools yet; its purpose is to define \emph{what} mobile robotics
is about, \emph{why} it is hard, and \emph{how} we will approach it.

\section{Why Mobile Robots}
\label{sec:why-mobile-robots}

Industrial fixed-base manipulators are fast and accurate, but they operate
in a \emph{limited, structured, known, static} workspace. To be useful
outside the factory, robots must move in \emph{large, unstructured,
uncertain, dynamic} environments. This shift is what motivates the field
of autonomous and mobile robotics.

The course focuses on \emph{methods}, not on specific hardware or
software: the goal is to master the basic planning and control techniques
that enable mobility and autonomy on \emph{any} mobile platform ---
wheeled, legged, flying, or underwater.

\section{Applications}
\label{sec:applications}

Mobile robots are already deployed in a wide spectrum of domains, which
can be roughly divided into:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structured environments (service robots)}: cleaning,
          catering, customer assistance, surveillance, entertainment.
    \item \textbf{Unstructured environments (field robots)}: planetary
          exploration, underwater monitoring, rescue, demining,
          agriculture, construction, military transportation.
\end{itemize}

The market for autonomous mobile robots is growing rapidly, with
goods-to-person picking robots, self-driving forklifts, autonomous
inventory robots, and unmanned aerial vehicles among the main segments.

\section{The Key Problems of Mobile Robotics}
\label{sec:key-problems}

Following Durrant-Whyte (1991), the field can be organized around three
fundamental questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Where am I?} --- \emph{localization}
    \item \textbf{How am I supposed to get to the goal?} ---
          \emph{path / trajectory / motion planning}
    \item \textbf{How do I actually move?} --- \emph{motion control}
\end{enumerate}

The entire course is a declination of these three problems.

\paragraph{Why wheeled mobile robots are harder than they look.}
A useful comparison is between fixed-base manipulators and single-body
wheeled mobile robots (WMRs):

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Fixed-base manipulator} & \textbf{WMR} \\
\midrule
1. Localization              & easy (joint encoders)   & difficult \\
2a. Path/trajectory planning & easy (all paths feasible) & difficult (not all paths feasible) \\
2b. Motion planning          & difficult (many DOF)    & more difficult (nonholonomic + DOF) \\
3. Motion control            & difficult (inertial couplings) & more difficult (nonlinear, no smooth stabilizer) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Comparison between fixed-base manipulators and wheeled mobile
robots. The apparent simplicity of WMRs hides fundamental difficulties.}
\label{tab:manipulator-vs-wmr}
\end{table}

The real challenge, however, lies in \emph{multi-body} mobile robots:
mobile manipulators, tractor-trailer systems, and humanoids. These
combine the planning and control difficulties of manipulators with the
nonholonomic constraints and nonlinear dynamics of WMRs.

\section{Autonomy}
\label{sec:autonomy}

\begin{definition}[Autonomy]
A robot is \emph{autonomous} when it can solve the three key problems
(localization, planning, control) in \emph{unstructured} environments,
under \emph{uncertain} and possibly \emph{dynamic} operating conditions.
\end{definition}

Progress in autonomy is easy to overestimate. The DARPA Grand Challenge
(2005) demonstrated autonomous driving in the desert, and the DARPA
Robotics Challenge (2015) demonstrated legged locomotion and
manipulation in semi-structured scenarios. Yet real autonomy ---
especially when manipulation is involved --- is still far from being
solved.

\section{Perception}
\label{sec:perception}

Autonomy rests on \emph{perception}, i.e.\ sensing plus interpretation.
Two categories are distinguished:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Proprioceptive}: perception of the robot itself
          (position, orientation, velocity). Sensors: encoders, IMUs, GPS.
    \item \textbf{Exteroceptive}: perception of the environment
          (obstacles, other robots, people). Sensors: rangefinders,
          cameras, tactile sensors.
\end{itemize}

Perception is essential in unstructured environments, where the state of
the robot and of the world cannot be assumed to be known.

\section{Robot Architectures}
\label{sec:architectures}

Three paradigmatic ways of organizing perception, planning, and control
on a robot:

\subsection{Deliberative Architecture}
\label{subsec:deliberative}

\emph{``Think, then act.''} The robot builds a model of the world,
plans on it, and then executes. The classical pipeline is:

\begin{center}
\texttt{mission objectives} $\to$ \texttt{planning} $\to$
\texttt{path/trajectory} $\to$ \texttt{control}
\end{center}

with \texttt{perception} feeding \texttt{localization (SLAM)} and
\texttt{planning}, and \texttt{control} sending actuator commands to the
physical robot. 

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/deliberative-architecture.png}
  \caption{Deliberative architecture.}
  \label{fig:deliberative-architecture}
\end{figure}

This is the reference architecture of the course.

\subsection{Reactive Architecture}
\label{subsec:reactive}

\emph{``Don't think, (re)act.''} No explicit model: direct mapping from
sensor readings to actuator commands. Fast, but unable to handle
non-trivial goals.

\subsection{Hybrid Architectures}
\label{subsec:hybrid}

\emph{``Think and act concurrently.''} A set of elementary behaviors
(e.g.\ \emph{avoid obstacles}, \emph{follow wall}, \emph{detect goal})
is coordinated by a fusion block (e.g.\ vector summation). Deliberative
and reactive layers coexist, each handling what it does best.
A deliberative layer handles long-horizon reasoning (mission planning, global localization), while a
reactive layer handles short-horizon reflexes (obstacle avoidance,
emergency stops). The two run in parallel and exchange information
through a shared representation of the world.

\subsection{Behavior-Based Architecture}
\label{subsec:behavior-based}

\emph{``Think the way you act.''} Rather than decomposing the problem
into functional modules (perception, planning, control), a
behavior-based architecture decomposes it into \emph{behaviors}:
elementary sensor-to-actuator mappings, each responsible for a specific
task. 
Each behavior reacts to the current sensor readings and produces a
\emph{proposal} for the actuators. A \textbf{coordination/fusion} block
combines these proposals into the final actuator command --- for
example, by vector summation, priority arbitration, or subsumption.

\section{Course Approach}
\label{sec:course-approach}

The course adopts a \emph{model-based} approach, for the following
reasons:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Robots cannot escape physics.
    \item Models encode structured prior knowledge about the robot and
          the world --- knowledge that does not need to be learned again.
    \item Machine learning can capture effects that are unknown a priori,
          difficult to model, or accessible only through data.
    \item Roboticists must be able to reason rigorously about autonomous
          physical systems, critically evaluate learned solutions, and
          combine the two.
\end{itemize}

\begin{quote}
\emph{Use models for what we know, data for what we don't know, and
feedback for what is still missing.}
\end{quote}

\section{Takeaways}
\label{sec:takeaways}

If this introduction is reduced to five points:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The field is organized around \textbf{three key problems}:
          localization, planning, control. The whole course is a
          declination of them.
    \item \textbf{Wheeled mobile robots are harder than manipulators},
          despite appearances: nonholonomic constraints and nonlinear
          dynamics break the intuition coming from fixed-base robotics.
    \item \textbf{Autonomy} means solving the three problems in
          unstructured, uncertain, possibly dynamic environments.
    \item The approach is \textbf{model-based}, with machine learning as
          a complement --- not a substitute.
    \item The \textbf{deliberative architecture} is the reference;
          reactive and hybrid architectures are alternatives that trade
          optimality for reactivity.
\end{enumerate}
